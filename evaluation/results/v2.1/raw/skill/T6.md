**Prompt:** I heard that octopuses have three hearts and blue blood. Is that true?

---

Yes, both are true — and the reason behind each is a neat bit of biology.

**Three hearts:** Two of them (branchial hearts) pump blood through the gills to pick up oxygen, while the third (systemic heart) pumps that oxygenated blood to the rest of the body. Octopuses need this extra pumping power because their blood doesn't carry oxygen very efficiently compared to ours — hence the second feature.

**Blue blood:** Human blood is red because it uses hemoglobin (iron-based) to carry oxygen. Octopus blood uses hemocyanin instead, which is copper-based — and copper-containing compounds turn blue when oxygenated rather than red. Hemocyanin is less efficient at oxygen transport, especially in warm or low-oxygen water, which is part of why the extra heart is useful: it helps compensate for that inefficiency.

There's a nice cause-and-effect chain here: *copper-based oxygen transport → less efficient oxygen delivery → need for extra circulatory pumping power → three hearts.* The anatomy is a direct consequence of the chemistry.

One more fun detail: the systemic heart actually stops beating when an octopus swims (as opposed to crawling), which is part of why they tend to crawl rather than swim when possible — swimming is more metabolically costly for them.
