**Prompt:** Why are manhole covers round?

---

The classic reason: a circle is the only shape that can't fall through its own opening, no matter how you tilt it.

Think about a square cover — its diagonal is longer than its side. If you tilt a square cover on edge, you can angle it just right to slip it diagonally down through its own hole. Same problem with rectangles, pentagons, hexagons — any shape with a "long axis" longer than its narrowest width can be rotated and dropped through. A circle has constant width in every direction (its diameter), so there's no orientation where it becomes narrower than the hole it sits in. It physically cannot fall in, even by accident.

A few other practical reasons reinforce the choice:

- **Easy to move**: a heavy iron disc can be rolled rather than dragged, which matters when the cover weighs 50–100+ kg.
- **No alignment needed**: a round cover drops onto a round frame in any rotation and seats correctly. A square one has to be rotated to match corners — more friction, more fumbling for a worker handling a heavy cast lid.
- **Manufacturing/stress reasons**: circular shapes distribute the load from traffic above more evenly around the rim, and casting a circle is simpler than casting sharp corners (which concentrate stress and are prone to cracking).

The "can't fall through" reason gets the most attention because it's a genuinely elegant piece of geometry — famously asked in interviews at places like Google not because manhole covers matter, but because it tests whether someone can reason from a physical constraint to a geometric principle (constant width) rather than just pattern-matching "round things roll well."

Interesting side note: circles aren't *uniquely* safe — shapes of "constant width" like the Reuleaux triangle (think of the rounded-triangle shape used in some rotary engines and drill bits) also can't fall through their own hole, even though they're not circular. Circles are just the simplest and cheapest version of that property to manufacture.
