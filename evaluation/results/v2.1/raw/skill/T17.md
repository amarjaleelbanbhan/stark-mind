**Prompt:** Why do we only use 10% of our brains?

---

That's actually a myth — we use virtually all of our brain, just not all of it at the exact same moment for the same task.

Here's what brain scans actually show: over the course of a day, you use 100% of your brain. Different regions specialize (vision, movement, language, emotion, memory), and they activate depending on what you're doing, but there's no dormant 90% sitting unused. Even during sleep, areas like the brainstem, cerebellum, and regions involved in memory consolidation stay busy. And evolutionarily, this makes sense — brain tissue is metabolically expensive (it burns about 20% of your resting energy despite being ~2% of body weight), so maintaining a mostly-idle organ would be a huge waste that evolution would have selected against.

The myth likely persists because it's a flattering idea — the notion that there's a vast reservoir of untapped potential is appealing (hence movies like *Lucy* and *Limitless*). It may also stem from a few older sources: early, now-outdated neuroscience that didn't yet understand "silent" cortical areas (which handle complex integrative work rather than simple motor/sensory signals), misattributed quotes from Einstein or William James, or confusion with the fact that *neurons* are a minority of brain cells by volume while glial cells make up the rest.

A useful related distinction: it's true that most *individual neurons* aren't firing at any given instant — but that's efficiency, not waste. A brain where every neuron fired constantly would be closer to a seizure than to genius.
