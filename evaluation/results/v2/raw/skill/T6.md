**Prompt:** I heard that octopuses have three hearts and blue blood. Is that true?

---

Yes, both are true, and both come from the same underlying problem: **moving oxygen efficiently without the help gravity and a land-based body give most animals.**

**The three hearts**

Two small "branchial" hearts sit right at the base of each gill, pumping blood specifically through the gills to pick up oxygen. A third, larger "systemic" heart then pushes that oxygenated blood out to the rest of the body. Octopuses need this extra pumping power because their blood carries oxygen far less efficiently than ours does — so the system compensates with more pumps rather than a stronger single one. Interestingly, the systemic heart actually stops beating when the octopus swims; jetting through the water squeezes the body in a way that works against that heart, which is part of why octopuses prefer crawling (more efficient) to swimming (exhausting) when they can.

**The blue blood**

This comes down to the molecule doing the oxygen-carrying. Our blood uses hemoglobin, an iron-based protein, which is red. Octopus blood uses hemocyanin, a copper-based protein, which is blue when oxygenated. Copper-based oxygen transport is actually worse than iron-based transport at normal temperatures and pressures — but it works notably better in cold, low-oxygen environments, like the deep, cold ocean water many octopus species inhabit. So the "upgrade" to three hearts is partly compensating for the "downgrade" in blood chemistry.

**The deeper pattern:** this is a nice case of a trade-off being patched by redundancy. A less efficient core component (hemocyanin) gets compensated for by added infrastructure (extra hearts) rather than by fixing the component itself — similar to how a slow database query sometimes gets solved by adding caching layers instead of rewriting the query.
