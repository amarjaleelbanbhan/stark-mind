## What actually happened at Tacoma Narrows (1940)

Here's the twist: the popular explanation you've probably heard — "the wind matched the bridge's natural frequency and caused resonance" — is **not quite right**, even though it's taught that way in a lot of textbooks and documentaries. I want to flag that explicitly, because it's one of the most persistent misconceptions in engineering education.

**What actually happened** is a phenomenon called **aeroelastic flutter**, and it's mechanistically different from forced resonance.

### Forced resonance vs. self-excited flutter

- **Forced resonance**: an external periodic force (like wind gusts at a steady frequency, or a crowd stomping) pushes a structure at a rate matching its natural frequency, and energy accumulates with each cycle. Push a swing at the right rhythm and it goes higher — the pusher is unaffected by the swing's motion.
- **Aeroelastic flutter**: the *motion of the structure itself* changes the aerodynamic forces acting on it, creating a feedback loop. The bridge moving gives the wind new angles of attack, which push the bridge further, which changes the angles of attack again. This is **self-excited** — no periodic forcing function is required. Steady wind is enough.

At Tacoma Narrows, the deck's solid, plate-girder sides (rather than an open truss) acted like a flat airfoil. As the deck twisted slightly in steady wind, vortices shed asymmetrically off the leading edge, creating a torque that twisted the deck further — and that twist changed the vortex shedding pattern again. This is the same instability category that causes airplane wing flutter: once wind speed crosses a critical threshold, aerodynamic damping goes *negative* — the wind stops resisting motion and starts amplifying it. Below that threshold, nothing happens. Above it, oscillation amplitude grows almost explosively, which matches contemporaries' accounts of the bridge "coming alive" in moderate (not extreme) wind, around 42 mph.

I'm reasonably confident in this account — it's the consensus engineering explanation (see the work of Robert Scanlan, who studied flutter extensively afterward) — but if you want the precise mathematical treatment (flutter derivatives, torsional-vertical mode coupling), that's a deeper aeroelasticity literature I'd want to point you toward rather than reconstruct from memory in full rigor.

## A few useful angles

**Structural design**: The original deck was unusually shallow and solid-sided for its span — it prioritized a sleek, light appearance (and lower cost) over aerodynamic stability. The replacement bridge used a deep, open-truss stiffening structure that lets wind pass through instead of pushing against a solid surface. That's the actual engineering fix: not making the bridge stronger, but making it aerodynamically "boring" to the wind — reducing its tendency to generate self-reinforcing vortices.

**Control systems framing**: Flutter is a textbook example of positive feedback crossing a stability threshold. Every oscillating or vibrating system you've ever dealt with — audio feedback squeal when a mic gets too close to a speaker, an op-amp circuit oscillating, even vocal cord flutter in speech — shares this structure: small perturbation → system property converts it into a force → force reinforces the perturbation → runaway, until something nonlinear (friction, saturation, or structural failure) caps it.

**A different bridge, a different mechanism — worth not conflating**: The London Millennium Bridge "wobble" in 2000 is often lumped in with Tacoma Narrows, but it's a *third* distinct mechanism: lateral (sideways) sway caused pedestrians to unconsciously synchronize their footsteps to stabilize themselves, and that synchronization — not wind — pumped energy into the bridge's lateral mode. That's **crowd-synchronized forced resonance with a feedback-driven forcing frequency** — genuinely resonance-like, but human-driven rather than aerodynamic. Three bridges, three different oscillation stories, often blurred into one "bridges resonate" narrative.

## The deeper pattern

The common thread isn't "bridges vibrate at their natural frequency" — it's that **any system with energy flowing through it can become unstable if some part of its response curve goes from damping to amplifying.** Wind flutter, pedestrian lockstep, and audio feedback all look superficially different but share: a feedback loop, a threshold, and a mode of motion that the structure happens to be "good at" doing.

## Takeaway

A compact mental model: **ask whether the energy source is independent of the structure's motion (forcing) or responsive to it (self-excited feedback).** That single question tells you whether the fix is "avoid matching a frequency" or "break the feedback loop" (e.g., by changing shape, adding dampers, or desynchronizing the driver — which is literally what London did by retrofitting dampers rather than changing pedestrian behavior).
