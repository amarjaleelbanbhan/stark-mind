**What might be happening?**

These are called "phantom traffic jams" or stop-and-go waves, and they're a well-studied phenomenon in traffic physics. No accident, no lane closure needed — they emerge from how humans drive.

Here's the mechanism: imagine a line of cars moving at a steady speed with small gaps. If one driver taps the brakes even slightly (phone glance, lane change, a bit of distraction), the driver behind reacts a beat later and brakes a bit harder to compensate. The next driver reacts even later and brakes harder still. This delay-plus-overcorrection compounds backward through the line until, several cars back, someone is braking hard enough to stop completely — even though the original "disturbance" was tiny and is long gone. The jam then propagates backward through traffic like a wave, often at a fairly consistent ~15-20 km/h, while individual cars pass through it, slow down, and speed up again. That's why it can seem to appear and vanish "for no reason" — the cause isn't a place, it's a density threshold being crossed.

**The deeper pattern**

This is a classic case of emergence from local rules: no single driver intends to create a jam, but the combination of reaction time + imperfect following distance produces a self-sustaining wave once traffic density passes a critical point. Below that density, small disturbances damp out. Above it, they amplify. It's mathematically similar to how fluids transition from smooth (laminar) flow to turbulence, and traffic engineers actually use fluid-dynamics-style models to describe it.

**One related fact worth knowing:** autonomous/adaptive cruise control cars that maintain more consistent following distances (and don't have human reaction lag) can dampen these waves instead of amplifying them — even a small percentage of such cars mixed into regular traffic has been shown experimentally to reduce stop-and-go oscillation.
