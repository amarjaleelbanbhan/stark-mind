**Prompt:** Things always seem to go wrong at the worst possible time.

---

There's actually a real question hiding inside that feeling, and the answer is a mix of two things: one real, one partly an illusion.

**The illusion part:** We tend to define "the worst possible time" *after* the failure happens. If your car breaks down on an ordinary Tuesday, you don't remember it as "the worst time" — you just remember a breakdown. But if it breaks down the morning of a job interview, that moment becomes memorable and gets labeled "the worst possible time," and it sticks in your memory far more vividly than the dozens of uneventful mornings. So part of this pattern is a reporting bias: high-stakes failures get flagged and recalled; low-stakes failures get forgotten. Your brain isn't lying, but it's sampling unevenly.

**The real part:** High-stakes moments often really do carry higher failure risk, for a non-mystical reason — they usually coincide with higher load, more complexity, and more last-minute changes. You push your laptop harder before a big presentation (more apps open, last-minute edits, maybe an update you installed the night before). You're more rushed and sleep-deprived before a big trip, which is when small mistakes (forgetting something, missing a turn) actually increase. Engineers see this too: systems fail more often under peak load, and peak load tends to line up with peak importance (a server is most likely to crash during the traffic spike that matters most, not during a quiet Tuesday). So stakes and failure probability aren't coincidentally correlated — stakes often *cause* the conditions that raise failure probability.

A useful mental model from engineering: build in margin *before* known high-load/high-stakes moments, and freeze changes right before them. Most "it broke at the worst time" stories include a last-minute tweak, an untested new element, or a system already running near its limit — the stakes didn't attract bad luck, they just removed the slack that would normally absorb a small problem.
