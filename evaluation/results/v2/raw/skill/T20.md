**Prompt:** What if the Moon were twice as close to Earth?

---

Distance changes don't affect everything on the Moon equally — different effects scale at very different rates, which is what makes this such a good "what if."

**Tides would get dramatically worse, not just moderately worse.** Tidal force scales with the *cube* of distance (1/r³), so halving the Earth-Moon distance means tidal forces roughly 8x stronger. Open-ocean tides that are now tens of centimeters could become meters, and the already-amplified tides in bays and estuaries (currently several meters in places like the Bay of Fundy) would likely be far more extreme — think regular coastal flooding events, not just "spring tide" curiosities.

**The Moon would look twice as big and eclipses would change character.** Angular size scales linearly with distance (1/r), so the Moon's apparent diameter would double, to roughly the size the Sun currently appears doubled. Since the Moon and Sun currently have almost the *same* angular size — which is why we get both total eclipses (Moon just barely covers the Sun) and annular eclipses (Moon slightly too small) — a Moon twice as large angularly would completely dominate the Sun. Annular eclipses would essentially disappear, and total eclipses would last much longer and sweep a wider path across Earth.

**The Moon would also move faster across the sky.** By Kepler's third law (T² ∝ r³), halving the distance cuts the orbital period to about 1/(2√2) of today's — roughly 9–10 days instead of 27.3. Full moons, tides, and moonrise times would all cycle noticeably faster within a month.

One honest caveat: I'm not fully confident giving you a precise "this corresponds to Earth X billion years ago" date, since the Moon's recession rate hasn't been constant over geologic time — but it's worth noting this isn't pure fantasy. The Moon actually *was* significantly closer in the distant past and has been slowly spiraling outward (~3.8 cm/year today) due to the very tidal interaction described above.

The deeper pattern here: small changes in a single variable (distance) can produce wildly different-magnitude effects depending on whether the underlying physics scales linearly, quadratically, or cubically. Tides (r⁻³) react violently to small distance changes; angular size (r⁻¹) reacts mildly by comparison.
