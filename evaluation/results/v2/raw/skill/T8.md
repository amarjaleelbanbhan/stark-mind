**Prompt:** Why are manhole covers round?

---

**What might be happening?**

The classic answer is geometric, not arbitrary: a circle is the one shape that *can't fall through its own hole*, no matter how you orient it. Tilt a square or rectangular cover on its diagonal and it will slip right through — the diagonal is longer than the side, so it's narrower than the hole at some angle. A circle has the same width in every direction, so there's no orientation where it becomes "narrower" than the opening. That's a nice safety feature when the cover is suspended over a multi-foot drop and a truck just drove over it.

**A few other practical reasons that reinforce the shape:**
- **Easy to move.** A round cover can be rolled on its edge instead of carried, which matters when it weighs 100+ lbs.
- **No orientation needed.** You never have to rotate it to line it up with the hole, unlike a square cover that must match corners.
- **Even stress distribution.** Round shapes handle the downward force of traffic more uniformly than corners, which concentrate stress.
- **Manufacturing.** A circular mold/cast is simpler and avoids weak points at corners.

**The deeper pattern**

This is a case of "shape constraints solving a safety problem for free." Rather than adding a lock or latch to prevent the cover from falling in, the geometry itself makes the failure mode impossible — a curve of constant width. Rotors in Wankel engines, certain coin shapes, and some mechanical linkages lean on the same "constant width" idea for similar reasons: eliminate an entire class of failure by shape choice rather than added mechanism.

One small nuance worth knowing: not *all* manhole covers are round — some are square or triangular but have a lip or frame that prevents them from being tilted into the hole, achieving the same safety goal through a different mechanism (a physical stop instead of geometry). So round is the elegant default, but it's not the only valid solution — just the one that needs the least extra engineering.
