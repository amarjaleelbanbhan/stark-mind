**Prompt:** I noticed my heart sometimes skips a beat when I drink coffee. Why does that happen?

---

**What might be happening:** Caffeine is a stimulant that affects the heart in a couple of ways. It blocks adenosine (a chemical that normally calms electrical activity in the heart), and it boosts adrenaline-like signaling. Both effects can make the heart's electrical system a little more "jumpy," occasionally triggering an early extra beat (called a premature atrial or ventricular contraction) followed by a pause — which is the "skip" you feel. The pause is actually the heart resetting its rhythm, not a beat that's truly missing.

**A few things that influence whether it happens:**
- **Dose and sensitivity** — some people's hearts are simply more reactive to caffeine's effect on adenosine receptors; this varies genetically.
- **Other amplifiers** — dehydration, low magnesium/potassium, poor sleep, stress, or an empty stomach can all make extra beats more likely, so coffee is sometimes the trigger that reveals an underlying sensitivity rather than the sole cause.
- **Normal baseline noise** — occasional skipped beats (PACs/PVCs) are extremely common even in healthy people with no stimulant involved; caffeine just tips the probability up.

**The deeper pattern:** this is a classic case of a system operating near a threshold — the heart's pacemaker cells already fire somewhat irregularly at a tiny scale, and a stimulant nudges the system just enough to occasionally cross into a perceptible, premature firing. Small input, visible output, because the system was already close to the edge.

**Worth knowing:** isolated skipped beats tied to caffeine are usually benign, but if you notice they're frequent, come with chest pain, dizziness, fainting, or a racing heart that doesn't settle, that's worth mentioning to a doctor rather than self-diagnosing — an ECG or Holter monitor can clarify what's actually happening electrically.

If you want, you could test it informally: note caffeine amount, timing, and whether the sensation correlates — a simple way to see if it's dose-dependent for you specifically.
