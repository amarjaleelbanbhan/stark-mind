**Prompt:** Why do old books smell different from new ones?

---

**What's happening:** Paper is mostly cellulose, but it also contains lignin, cotton/wood fibers, and chemical residues from manufacturing (bleach, sizing agents, adhesives). Over years, these compounds slowly break down — oxidize, hydrolyze — releasing volatile organic compounds into the air. That "old book smell" is largely a mix of:

- **Vanillin** and similar compounds from lignin breakdown — gives a sweet, almost vanilla-like note
- **Benzaldehyde** — faintly almond-like
- **Toluene and ethylbenzene** — slightly sweet, floral
- **Furans and other oxidation products** — contribute musty, earthy undertones

New books smell different mainly because those breakdown reactions haven't happened yet — you're smelling fresh ink solvents, fresh glue, and uncured paper chemicals instead, which is a sharper, more chemical scent.

**The deeper pattern:** this is slow, continuous chemical decay releasing byproducts as a scent signature — similar to how wine or whiskey develops aroma compounds through aging (oxidation again), or how "new car smell" is off-gassing from plastics and adhesives that fades over time. In all these cases, smell is really a readout of a material's chemical history.

One mildly surprising wrinkle: paper quality affects *how* a book ages. Cheap wood-pulp paper (common since the mid-1800s) has more lignin and acidic residue, so it yellows and smells more strongly over decades. Older rag-based paper (pre-1800s) often ages more slowly and smells different — less sweet, more "dusty."

If you want, I could suggest a quick way to notice this yourself — comparing the smell of an old paperback vs. an old hardcover with rag paper.
