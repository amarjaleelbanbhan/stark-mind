**Prompt:** Why do we only use 10% of our brains?

---

Good one to question — this is actually a myth, not a finding. We don't only use 10% of our brains.

**What's actually true:** Brain imaging (fMRI, PET scans) shows that over the course of a day, you use virtually all of your brain. Different regions specialize in different tasks — vision, movement, language, memory, emotion — and at any given moment, most areas show *some* activity, even during sleep. There's no dormant 90% waiting to be unlocked.

**Where the myth likely comes from:**
- A garbled version of neuroscience findings that only ~10% of brain cells are *neurons* — the rest are glial cells, which support and protect neurons rather than directly process information. People misread "10% of cells do most of the signaling" as "10% of the brain is used."
- Early, crude brain-mapping techniques (before modern imaging) found specific functions for some regions but not others, leading to false conclusions that the "silent" areas were unused — they just weren't understood yet.
- It's also just a flattering idea: it implies untapped potential, which makes it great marketing fuel for self-help books, "brain training" apps, and movies like *Lucy* or *Limitless*.

**The deeper pattern:** This is a case of a scientific half-truth (glial cells vs. neurons) getting flattened into a catchy but false claim, then surviving because it's emotionally appealing rather than because it's accurate. That's a useful thing to watch for generally — claims that feel inspiring or dramatic spread faster than the more boring, accurate version ("your brain is fully active, just specialized and efficient").

**Takeaway:** A good mental model here is "efficient and specialized" rather than "idle." Brain damage to even small, specific areas (a stroke, for instance) usually causes very noticeable deficits — which wouldn't happen if 90% of the brain were just spare capacity.
